About Tripwire

THE RECORD OF THE WORLD IS QUIETLY CHANGING.

Pages have been vanishing. Articles are getting rewritten. Books are getting edited between editions, and videos are known to disappear. Most of the time, nobody notices. Tripwire notices and keeps a copy for you.

Who it's for

  • Indigenous communities, and the people who document them. Their knowledge is thin online, and what exists is easy to rewrite, poison or delete.
  • Independent journalists, storytellers and archivists, whose work lives on platforms that can remove it overnight.
  • Researchers, librarians and Wikipedia editors who need to know what a source said before it changed.
  • Anyone whose subject is easy to overlook: a minority language, a local history, a person who isn't famous.

Why it exists

“I want to ensure that Indigenous knowledge does not just die with the people who have this knowledge. My goal was to see as much of this information documented, preserved, and archived in its most raw, sacred, and most honest formats. There should always be a point of reference that cannot be poisoned with a few prompts. The truth of this life should live beyond our innovation.”
Purple, Tripwire's maker

As AI learns from the internet, whatever survives online starts to count as the truth. Communities with little written about them risk being left out, or misrepresented by whoever edits last. Tripwire is a small tool against that: it watches the record, and it keeps the receipts.

What it does

  • Watches Wikipedia articles (in any language) and any web page, grouped into themes or as single links, once a day.
  • Keeps the before and after of every change, sorts it from a tiny typo to a large removal, and flags names spelled differently.
  • Watches videos on YouTube and TikTok for removals, private videos, age restrictions and new titles.
  • Compares two editions of a book or document word by word: swapped words, renamed people and places, removed passages, plus facts about both editions. Scanned PDFs, like gazettes and memos, are read on your own device: the document never leaves it.
  • My shelf: your own copies of gazettes, memos, photos of documents and web pages, searchable, and Take my shelf with me: download the whole shelf as one file, a ZIP for any browser or a file for Kiwix, the free app that reads whole libraries with no internet.
  • Then and now: compares a Wikipedia article today with Kiwix's dated offline copy of Wikipedia, and any other page with the Internet Archive's copy from about a year ago.
  • Live watch: keeps an eye on a fast-moving page, like a live news page, while you have it open, and beeps when something new appears.
  • Keeps public copies with the Internet Archive, only when you choose.
  • Sends one calm summary (daily, weekly or monthly, by email if you like) instead of a ping for every change.
  • For everyone: works with screen readers, can read every page aloud (for people who can't see well or can't read), has a calm mode, bigger text and strong contrast, and is starting to speak Hindi and Sesotho (drafts, with help welcome).

Deliberate choices

No AI inside, on purpose.
Tripwire compares texts with plain, fixed rules. It gives the same answer every time, it's free to run, and it can't make things up, which matters for a tool about keeping the record honest.
Public copies are asked for twice.
A copy at the Internet Archive is public and very hard to remove. Tripwire only offers one for big changes, asks twice, and reminds you that knowledge a community keeps private or sacred may be better left uncopied.
A summary, not a ping for every change.
Alerts in the middle of your day mostly cause panic. One summary a week (or a day, or a month: you choose) is enough to notice what matters.
Names are watched closely.
A person's or place's name spelled differently can mean a language is spreading, or fading. Tripwire flags it.
Book files are deleted after 7 days.
Only the comparison is kept. Copyrighted books stay private to you.
Privacy by default.
No ads, no selling data. Fonts are served by Tripwire itself, so visitors' browsers don't contact a font company. The site counts anonymous visits with Vercel Web Analytics, which uses no cookies.
No account needed.
You can use everything without signing up. What you add lasts until you close your browser (and is deleted a few days later); make an account only if you want to keep it.
Our own human check.
Signing up asks a question in words and does a little maths in the background, with no outside service watching you. No check stops a determined AI; this one makes automated sign-ups slow and costly.
Honest numbers.
The Stats page says what it can't count: books printed locally without an ISBN or a library record, as often happens in Lesotho and many other places, aren't in anyone's totals.
Slow on purpose.
Text types itself out, and Books pauses for a moment before it opens. Preservation is patient work.

What it can't do (yet)

  • TikTok doesn't publicly say when a video is marked sensitive, so Tripwire can't see that.
  • YouTube sometimes blocks servers from reading video pages; Tripwire then knows less (but never reports a false removal).
  • Scanned pages are read with an English (Latin-alphabet) reader, so Sesotho and other languages work, but letters with accents or special marks may sometimes be misread.
  • The Internet Archive can only save a page as it is now. Once a page has changed, the old version can only be saved if it still exists somewhere (Wikipedia keeps every version; most websites don't).

How Tripwire changed

Built in two days at the Edge City Buildathon (October 2026), in response to its purpose and to what didn't work.

  1. The idea: watch for edits to information about Indigenous communities.
  2. Alerts → a weekly summary. A ping for every edit would just make people panic. Now it's one calm summary.
  3. Automatic Internet Archive copies → asked twice. Copies are public and permanent, and some knowledge shouldn't be public. Now Tripwire only offers, and only for big changes.
  4. Themes only → single links too. Sometimes you care about one page, not a whole topic.
  5. Added videos, because so much independent journalism and storytelling now lives on YouTube and TikTok.
  6. Added books, after hearing of books being quietly reworded between editions.
  7. A cluttered list of changes → a chart, highlights and book facts. The first version was a wall of text.
  8. Fake changes from Project Gutenberg's licence text → ignored. Two identical books looked different.
  9. Open Library only → plus a world estimate. One catalogue leans towards Western libraries; the page now says what no one can count.
  10. Going live: fixed a Vercel “not found” error and a missing database, and a bug the tests caught before anyone saw it.
  11. A plain look → a retro computer that slows you down a little, on purpose. Then all green, so it feels like one place.
  12. Scanned PDFs → read on your own device, because so much official paperwork (gazettes, memos) is scanned, and some of it quietly disappears from government websites.
  13. Live watching for pages that change by the minute.
  14. Built for sighted readers of English → for everyone: read aloud, screen-reader support, calm mode, and the first Hindi and Sesotho drafts, open for others to translate.
  15. Copies you keep yourself: My shelf, downloadable as a ZIP or a Kiwix file, because a copy on a government website, or on Tripwire, isn't the same as a copy in your own hands. And Then and now, comparing Wikipedia with Kiwix's offline copy.
  16. A sign-up wall → try it first. Being made to create an account before seeing anything makes most people leave. Now anyone can use Tripwire straight away; an account is only for keeping things after you close the browser, and for email summaries.
  17. One summary a week → you choose: daily, weekly or monthly.
  18. A front door. Opening Tripwire should feel like discovering a world, the way libraries and the early internet did. So it opens with falling binary and a purple rabbit to follow.
