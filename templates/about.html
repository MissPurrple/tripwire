About Tripwire

THE RECORD OF THE WORLD IS QUIETLY CHANGING.

Pages have been vanishing. Articles are getting rewritten. Books are getting edited between editions, and videos are known to disappear. Most of the time, nobody notices. Tripwire notices and keeps a copy for you.

Who it's for

  • Indigenous communities, and the people who document them. Their knowledge is thin online, and what exists is easy to rewrite, poison or delete.
  • Independent journalists, storytellers and archivists, whose work lives on platforms that can remove it overnight.
  • Researchers, librarians and Wikipedia editors who need to know what a source said before it changed.
  • Anyone whose subject is easy to overlook: a minority language, a local history, a person who isn't famous.

Why it exists

“I want to ensure that Indigenous knowledge does not just die with the people who have this knowledge. My goal was to see as much of this information documented, preserved, and archived in its most raw, sacred, and most honest formats. There should always be a point of reference that cannot be poisoned with a few prompts. The truth of this life should live beyond our innovation.”
Purple, Tripwire's maker

As AI learns from the internet, whatever survives online starts to count as the truth. Communities with little written about them risk being left out, or misrepresented by whoever edits last. Tripwire is a small tool against that: it watches the record, and it keeps the receipts.

Why not just use what already exists?

  • Other tools tell you that something changed. Tripwire keeps your own copy. Wikipedia already has watchlists and email alerts, and services like Visualping and changedetection.io already watch web pages for changes. They watch something change. Tripwire keeps your own copy of what it said before, notices what kind of change it was (down to a person's or a place's name spelled differently), and lets you carry it all offline, in one file that's yours.
  • For communities that aren't fully online, it can be the preservation tool. Scanned PDFs that circulate from phone to phone, gazettes and memos that are never publicly documented or archived, and places where archiving isn't done well to begin with: Tripwire reads them, keeps them, and lets a community hold its own record, on its own devices, even with no internet.

What it does

  • Watches Wikipedia articles (in any language), any web page and videos, grouped into themes or as single links, once a day. Live watch, at the top of the Watching page, keeps an eye on a fast-moving page, like a live news page, while you have it open, and beeps when something new appears.
  • Keeps the before and after of every change, sorts it from a tiny typo to a large removal, and flags names spelled differently.
  • Books & videos: compares two editions of a book or document word by word (swapped words, renamed people and places, removed passages, plus facts about both editions), and checks YouTube and TikTok videos for removals, private videos, age restrictions and new titles. Scanned PDFs, like gazettes and memos, are read on your own device: the document never leaves it. A book's link, or a video, can then be watched.
  • My shelf: your own copies of gazettes, memos, photos of documents and web pages, searchable, and Take my shelf with me: download the whole shelf as one file, a ZIP for any browser or a file for Kiwix, the free app that reads whole libraries with no internet. Your public copies at the Internet Archive are listed there too: made only when you choose. Every copy gets a signed stamp of originality: where it came from, when, whether Tripwire fetched it itself or its owner uploaded it, and a fingerprint of the copy. Anyone can check a stamp.
  • Test against Kiwix, and against the Internet Archive: two buttons next to anything you watch. They compare it today with an older copy nobody can quietly edit: Kiwix's dated offline copy of Wikipedia, or the Internet Archive's copy from about a year ago. If there's no exact copy, Tripwire offers similar ones (an article under an older name, or nearby pages on the same site).
  • One calm summary (daily, weekly or monthly, by email if you like) at the top of Changes, instead of a ping for every change.
  • For everyone: works with screen readers, can read every page aloud (for people who can't see well or can't read), and has a calm mode, bigger text and strong contrast.

Deliberate choices

No AI inside, on purpose.
Tripwire compares texts with plain, fixed rules. It gives the same answer every time, it's free to run, and it can't make things up, which matters for a tool about keeping the record honest.
Public copies are asked for twice.
A copy at the Internet Archive is public and very hard to remove. Tripwire only offers one for big changes, asks twice, and reminds you that knowledge a community keeps private or sacred may be better left uncopied.
A summary, not a ping for every change.
Alerts in the middle of your day mostly cause panic. One summary a week (or a day, or a month: you choose) is enough to notice what matters.
Names are watched closely.
A person's or place's name spelled differently can mean a language is spreading, or fading. Tripwire flags it.
Book files are deleted after 7 days.
Only the comparison is kept. Copyrighted books stay private to you.
Privacy by default.
No ads, no selling data. Fonts are served by Tripwire itself, so visitors' browsers don't contact a font company. The site counts anonymous visits with Vercel Web Analytics, which uses no cookies.
Your own vault, locked on your device.
Shelf items you lock are scrambled in your browser (AES-256-GCM) before they're sent, with a key only you hold. Tripwire stores them but can't read them, and they leave with you, still locked, in your download. No back door.
No account needed.
You can use everything without signing up. What you add lasts until you close your browser (and is deleted a few days later); make an account only if you want to keep it.
Our own human check.
Signing up asks a question in words and does a little maths in the background, with no outside service watching you. No check stops a determined AI; this one makes automated sign-ups slow and costly.
Honest numbers.
The Stats page says what it can't count: books printed locally without an ISBN or a library record, as often happens in Lesotho and many other places, aren't in anyone's totals.
Slow on purpose.
Text types itself out, the opening takes its time, and Books & videos pauses for a moment before it opens. Preservation is patient work.
Stamps that tell the truth.
A stamp says “fetched by Tripwire” only when Tripwire fetched the copy itself. An uploaded file is stamped as uploaded: Tripwire can prove when it was kept and that it hasn't changed, but not where it came from, and it says so. Stamps are signed, so they can't be made or edited by anyone else, and they can be checked without Tripwire.
Languages only once they're checked.
A translation goes on the site only after a native speaker has checked it. A wrong translation, on a tool about keeping the record honest, would be its own small rewrite.

What it can't do (yet)

  • No website can promise perfect safety. Tripwire keeps your things private to your account, but please don't keep very personal documents here (identity papers, medical or bank records, passwords, private family papers).
  • TikTok doesn't publicly say when a video is marked sensitive, so Tripwire can't see that.
  • YouTube sometimes blocks servers from reading video pages; Tripwire then knows less (but never reports a false removal).
  • Scanned pages are read with an English (Latin-alphabet) reader, so Sesotho and other languages work, but letters with accents or special marks may sometimes be misread.
  • The Internet Archive can only save a page as it is now. Once a page has changed, the old version can only be saved if it still exists somewhere (Wikipedia keeps every version; most websites don't).

What we'd like to see next

  • Tripwire in Hindi and Sesotho. First drafts exist, but they stay off the site until a native speaker has checked them. If you speak Hindi or Sesotho and would like to help, please get in touch or send a pull request on GitHub. Other languages are welcome too.
  • A voice that can read Sesotho aloud, and a reader for scanned pages that knows Sesotho's spelling, so people who can't read, or can't see well, can use Tripwire in their own language.
  • A “Watch this with Tripwire” browser button: one click to watch the page you're on.

How Tripwire changed

Built in two days at the Edge City Buildathon (October 2026), in response to its purpose and to what didn't work.

  1. The idea: watch for edits to information about Indigenous communities.
  2. Alerts → a weekly summary. A ping for every edit would just make people panic. Now it's one calm summary.
  3. Automatic Internet Archive copies → asked twice. Copies are public and permanent, and some knowledge shouldn't be public. Now Tripwire only offers, and only for big changes.
  4. Themes only → single links too. Sometimes you care about one page, not a whole topic.
  5. Added videos, because so much independent journalism and storytelling now lives on YouTube and TikTok.
  6. Added books, after hearing of books being quietly reworded between editions.
  7. A cluttered list of changes → a chart, highlights and book facts. The first version was a wall of text.
  8. Fake changes from Project Gutenberg's licence text → ignored. Two identical books looked different.
  9. Open Library only → plus a world estimate. One catalogue leans towards Western libraries; the page now says what no one can count.
  10. Going live: fixed a Vercel “not found” error and a missing database, and a bug the tests caught before anyone saw it.
  11. A plain look → a retro computer that slows you down a little, on purpose. Then all green, so it feels like one place.
  12. Scanned PDFs → read on your own device, because so much official paperwork (gazettes, memos) is scanned, and some of it quietly disappears from government websites.
  13. Live watching for pages that change by the minute.
  14. Built for sighted readers of English → for everyone: read aloud, screen-reader support and calm mode. First Hindi and Sesotho drafts were written too, then taken off the site until native speakers can check them.
  15. Copies you keep yourself: My shelf, downloadable as a ZIP or a Kiwix file, because a copy on a government website, or on Tripwire, isn't the same as a copy in your own hands. And buttons to test what you watch against Kiwix's offline copy of Wikipedia and the Internet Archive.
  16. A sign-up wall → try it first. Being made to create an account before seeing anything makes most people leave. Now anyone can use Tripwire straight away; an account is only for keeping things after you close the browser, and for email summaries.
  17. One summary a week → you choose: daily, weekly or monthly.
  18. A front door. Opening Tripwire should feel like discovering a world, the way libraries and the early internet did. So it opens with falling binary and a purple rabbit to follow.
  19. Eleven pages → five. An honest review said there was too much to take in. Now: Watching (with Live watch and videos inside it), My shelf (with public copies), Changes (with the summary), Books & videos side by side, and About. Stats moved to the bottom of every page, as a fun extra.
  20. A comparison against the wrong page → checks that refuse it. Testing an article against Kiwix once compared it with a few dozen words of a “prove you're not a robot” page. Now Tripwire reads Kiwix's copies exactly as stored, checks the page really is the article, refuses copies that are suspiciously short, shows the start of both versions, and, if Kiwix's site won't let it in, offers Wikipedia's own saved version from the same date instead.
  21. Copies anyone could fake → signed stamps of originality. A copy is only useful as evidence if people can trust where and when it was taken.
